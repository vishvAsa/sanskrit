I think it derives from an earlier Near Eastern abjad closer to Phoenician and spread via trade contact with peninsular India.

## Adaption
The existence of Kharoṣṭhī in the Northwest would be redundant if both scripts descended from Aramaic script and the Indus (and Indus grafitti) connection remains highly doubtful and speculative.

How would you account for the rapid spread of Brāhmī to Sri Lanka (Anurādhapura) and Southern India (Kīḻaḍi) by around 450 BC?

The Persians only adopted the Imperial Aramaic script & language as their official administrative medium around 500 BC, which is rather narrow time frame for adoption and modification (to accomodate native phonology) across the Indian subcontinent.

Moreover, how would you account for letters like Brāhmī 𑀥 which are seemingly closer to Phoenician than the "y-like" shape of Aramaic?

Moreover, how would you explain the redundancy with Kharoṣṭhī in the Northwest?

Kharoṣṭhī clearly descends from Aramaic as the letter shapes are much closer to Aramaic and the script is RtL, unlike Brāhmī (LtR).

Back when the general consensus was top-down imposition during the Mauryan period, the Aramaic theory was stronger, though the evolution of most letter shapes remained unexplained.

We do know that trade existed between the Near East and Indian ports along the Western Coast like Bharukaccha, so the solution which would best fit the existing data would be to assume the existence a transitional script (between Phoenician and Aramaic) which was adopted via Maritime Trade Networks some time around the 6th or possibly even 7th century BC.
